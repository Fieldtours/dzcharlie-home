POINTE DU HOC

One stop, exactly as the guide gives it to you. Every guide has hundreds like it.
NORMANDY, FRANCE · 6–8 JUNE 1944Allow 1 hrHIGH exact spot known

What happened

German guns on this 35-metre headland between Utah and Omaha were thought to threaten both beaches. On 6 June 1944, 225 men of the 2nd Ranger Battalion under Lieutenant Colonel James Earl Rudder landed late, at about 07:10, and climbed the cliffs with rocket-fired grapnels and ladders under fire. At the top they found the casemates empty: the guns had been moved inland. Patrols found five of the six in an orchard and wrecked them with thermite grenades by about 09:00. The Rangers then held the headland against counter-attacks until relieved on 8 June, by when only about 90 were still fit to fight.

What you see today

The ground is still a moonscape of bomb craters around ruined casemates and the observation bunker at the cliff edge, topped by the granite Ranger Monument. Look over the edge at the cliffs the Rangers climbed.

Where accounts differA popular myth says the assault was pointless because the guns had gone. In fact the guns were nearby and could still reach the beaches, and the Rangers found and disabled them on the morning of D-Day.

Visiting

Free; open daily 09:00–17:00. Works by the American Battle Monuments Commission run until mid-2027: the visitor centre and main car park (P1) are closed, and some paths and bunkers are fenced off. Access is by the west path only, with temporary parking signposted.

Checked October 2026. Check the website before you go.

WHO MAKES THIS · HOW WE RESEARCH

CHECKED, SOURCED, CORRECTABLE

History deserves care. Here is who is behind the guides and how every stop is put together.
DZ

Who we are

DZ Charlie is developed by a group of serious enthusiasts. The guides are free, with no ads and no sign-up, and the site sets no cookies.

The name comes from Drop Zone C in Normandy, where Easy Company's war began on the night of 5–6 June 1944, and where the first guide starts.

How every stop is researched

  1. From opened sources. Each stop is written from sources read for that stop: the site's own museum or memorial pages, Wikipedia in several languages, TracesOfWar and local history sites.
  2. Pins checked twice. The exact spot comes from a published position. In Footsteps of WWII, Into Europe and the Spanish Civil War every pin was then checked again against OpenStreetMap, and pins that were off were moved.
  3. Disputes shown, not hidden. Where sources disagree on numbers, dates or who did what, the stop says so.
  4. Visiting info dated. Prices, opening times and closures are checked and dated, because they change.

How sure we are

  • HIGH Exact spot known: a landmark, museum, cemetery, memorial, bridge or building, or a position fixed by the records.
  • MEDIUM Well supported, but the exact spot is uncertain.
  • LOW General area only: the pin is the centre of a search area.

Main sources

American Battle Monuments Commission · Volksbund (German war graves) · Auschwitz-Birkenau Memorial · regional heritage services · museum and memorial websites · Wikipedia · TracesOfWar · OpenStreetMap. Each guide lists its sources.
